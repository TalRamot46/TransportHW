\section{Introduction \& Case's Method}

This report presents the solutions to Homework Assignment 1, which focuses on the monoenergetic, steady-state Boltzmann transport equation in slab geometry for an infinite, homogeneous, isotropic medium with an isotropic plane delta-source at $x = 0$. The transport equation is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \mu \frac{\partial \psi(x, \mu)}{\partial x} + \psi(x, \mu) = \frac{c}{2} \int_{-1}^1 \psi(x, \mu') d\mu' + \frac{1}{4\pi} \delta(x)
\end{equation}
where $\psi(x, \mu)$ is the angular flux, $c \in [0, 1)$ is the scattering ratio, and $\mu$ is the cosine of the angle of the particle's velocity with the $x$-axis.

In 1953, Kenneth Case analyzed this equation by searching for separation-of-variables solutions of the form:
\begin{equation}
    \psi_\nu(x, \mu) = e^{-x/\nu} \phi_\nu(\mu)
\end{equation}
Substituting this ansatz into the homogeneous transport equation leads to the eigenvalue problem:
\begin{equation}
    (\nu - \mu) \phi_\nu(\mu) = \frac{c\nu}{2} \int_{-1}^1 \phi_\nu(\mu') d\mu'
\end{equation}
Normalizing the eigenfunctions such that $\int_{-1}^1 \phi_\nu(\mu') d\mu' = 1$, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    (\nu - \mu) \phi_\nu(\mu) = \frac{c\nu}{2}
\end{equation}

Depending on the value of the eigenvalue $\nu$, two cases arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Discrete Spectrum ($\nu \notin [-1, 1]$):} Since $\nu - \mu \neq 0$ for all $\mu \in [-1, 1]$, we can divide directly by $\nu - \mu$, yielding the discrete eigenfunctions:
    \begin{equation}
        \phi_0^{\pm}(\mu) = \frac{c\nu_0}{2(\nu_0 \mp \mu)}
    \end{equation}
    Integrating these over $[-1, 1]$ and applying the normalization condition yields the transcendental characteristic equation for the discrete eigenvalues $\pm\nu_0$:
    \begin{equation}
        1 = c\nu_0 \text{arctanh}(1/\nu_0)
    \end{equation}
    For $c \in (0, 1)$, there is exactly one pair of real, positive roots $\pm\nu_0$ with $\nu_0 > 1$.

    \item \textbf{Continuous Spectrum ($\nu \in [-1, 1]$):} In this case, the term $\nu - \mu$ vanishes at $\mu = \nu$. Thus, we cannot divide by $\nu - \mu$ in the sense of ordinary functions. Instead, in the theory of distributions (generalized functions), the general solution to the equation is:
    \begin{equation}
        \phi_\nu(\mu) = \frac{c\nu}{2} \mathcal{P} \frac{1}{\nu - \mu} + \lambda(\nu)\delta(\mu - \nu)
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{P}$ represents the Cauchy principal value distribution, $\delta(\mu - \nu)$ is the Dirac delta function, and $\lambda(\nu)$ is the dispersion function. Integrating $\phi_\nu(\mu)$ over $\mu \in [-1, 1]$ and setting the integral to 1 determines $\lambda(\nu)$:
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda(\nu) = 1 - c\nu \text{arctanh}(\nu)
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Intuition Behind the Continuous Spectrum and Generalized Functions}

The presence of the Dirac delta function $\delta(\mu - \nu)$ and the principal value term $\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{\nu-\mu}$ in the continuous eigenfunctions $\phi_\nu(\mu)$ can feel counter-intuitive. 

Physically, the continuous spectrum represents the singular behavior of particles that travel without undergoing collisions (uncollided transport) or undergo highly directional scattering. A particle traveling at a specific direction $\mu = \nu$ represents a singular distribution in angle, which is modeled by the Dirac delta $\delta(\mu - \nu)$. The principal value distribution $\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{\nu - \mu}$ accounts for the strong coupling between nearby angles due to scattering, where the singularity is balanced symmetrically.

However, when we look at the scalar flux $\phi(x)$, which is the angle-integrated quantity:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = 2\pi \int_{-1}^1 \psi(x, \mu) d\mu
\end{equation}
these angular singularities are integrated out. Under integration, the distribution $\phi_\nu(\mu)$ acts as a linear functional mapping to a constant:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-1}^1 \phi_\nu(\mu) d\mu = 1
\end{equation}
This leads to the transient component of the scalar flux:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{tr}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 \frac{e^{-|x|/\nu}}{N_\nu} d\nu
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{equation}
    N_\nu = \nu \left[ \lambda^2(\nu) + \frac{\pi^2 c^2 \nu^2}{4} \right]
\end{equation}

Crucially, \textbf{no distributions or generalized functions remain in the scalar flux integral}. The integrand is a regular, smooth function of $\nu$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$. For any $x > 0$, as $\nu \to 0$, the exponential term $e^{-|x|/\nu}$ decays extremely rapidly, making the integrand vanish at 0. As $\nu \to 1$, the dispersion function $\lambda(\nu) \to -\infty$, which causes the normalization factor $N_\nu \to \infty$ and the integrand to vanish at 1. Thus, the numerical integration is highly well-behaved.

\section{Question 2: Numerical Code for Diffusion Equation}

To solve the time-independent diffusion equation with a delta-source at $x=0$ numerically, we exploit the symmetry of the medium and integrate over the half-domain $x \in [-a, 0]$. Since the delta-source is located at $x=0$, the domain $x < 0$ is source-free, governed by the homogeneous equation:
\begin{equation}
    - D \frac{d^2 \phi(x)}{dx^2} + (1 - c) \phi(x) = 0
\end{equation}

\subsection{Analytical Conversion to a First-Order System}
We convert this second-order ODE to a linear system of two first-order ODEs. Let:
\begin{equation}
    y_1(x) = \phi(x), \quad y_2(x) = \frac{d\phi(x)}{dx}
\end{equation}
This yields the first-order system:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dx} \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y_2 \\ \frac{1-c}{D} y_1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Boundary Conditions and the Shooting Method}
Because the exact solution decays exponentially as $e^{-\kappa |x|}$ where $\kappa = \sqrt{(1-c)/D}$, we choose the truncation boundary $x = -a$ where $a = 3/\kappa$ (corresponding to 3 decay lengths, where the flux is negligible).

We set the function boundary condition at the far boundary to zero:
\begin{equation}
    y_1(-a) = 0
\end{equation}
and parameterize the unknown initial derivative as a shooting parameter $s$:
\begin{equation}
    y_2(-a) = s
\end{equation}

To find the correct $s$, we use the derivative jump condition at the source. Integrating the governing equation $-D\phi''(x) + (1-c)\phi(x) = \delta(x)$ from $-\epsilon$ to $+\epsilon$ and taking the limit $\epsilon \to 0$ yields:
\begin{equation}
    - D \left[ \phi'(0^+) - \phi'(0^-) \right] = 1
\end{equation}
By symmetry of the infinite-medium solution ($\phi(x) = \phi(-x) \implies \phi'(0^-) = - \phi'(0^+)$), this simplifies to the target boundary condition at $x=0$:
\begin{equation}
    y_2(0^-) = \phi'(0^-) = \frac{1}{2D}
\end{equation}

Thus, we define the objective mismatch function:
\begin{equation}
    f(s) = y_2(0; s) - \frac{1}{2D}
\end{equation}
where $y_2(0; s)$ is obtained by integrating the first-order system from $-a$ to $0$ with the initial state $[0.0, s]^T$. We use a bisection-based root-finding algorithm (specifically Brent's method, `brentq`) to solve $f(s^*) = 0$ for the optimal initial derivative $s^*$.

\subsection{Comparison with the Analytical Solution}
The numerical solution is compared to the analytical classical diffusion solution:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{class}}(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{D(1-c)}} e^{\kappa x} \quad (x < 0)
\end{equation}
for three values of the scattering ratio: $c = 0.5, 0.7, 0.9$. The comparison of the flux values and the relative error are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:num_vs_ana_diff} and Figure~\ref{fig:num_diff_errors}.
