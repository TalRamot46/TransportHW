\section*{Question 1 - Useful identity for the solution of the time-dependent slab-geometry Boltzmann equation}
In this question we are asked to prove the following identity for the time-dependent slab-geometry green function:
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x,\mu,t;c) = ce^{-(1-c){v\Sigma_t} t}\psi(cx,\mu,ct;1) \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
We will do it in two parts. First, we will write down the equation that $\tilde{\psi} = \psi(cx,\mu,ct;1)$ satisfies, and then we will use it in order to prove that the product $ce^{-(1-c){v\Sigma_t} t}\tilde{\psi}$ satisfies the same equation as $\psi(x,\mu,t;c)$.
\subsection*{The equation for $\tilde{\psi}$}
\paragraph{Notation:} We will denote by $\Psi(X,\mu,T)\equiv\psi(X,\mu,T;1)$ the $c=1$ solution written in its own variables, and by $\Psi_T$ and $\Psi_X$ its partial derivatives with respect to them, so that $\tilde{\psi}(x,\mu,t) = \Psi(cx,\mu,ct)$ with $X=cx$ and $T=ct$. \textbf{This notation is used to avoid confusion when using the chain rule, as will be done immediately.}
First, $\Psi$ satisfies the $c=1$ equation in the variables $X$ and $T$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\Psi_T + \mu \Psi_X + \Sigma_t \Psi = \frac{\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \Psi d\mu^\prime + \delta(x)\delta(t)
\end{equation}
which after applying the chain rule, $\Psi_T = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial t}$ and $\Psi_X = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial x}$, and multiplying by $c$, becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + c\Sigma_t \tilde{\psi} = \frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \tilde{\psi}d\mu^\prime \label{eq:tilde} + c\delta(x)\delta(t)
\end{equation}
So $\tilde{\psi}$ obeys the same equation as $\psi(x,\mu,t;c)$ except that it is removed at the rate $c\Sigma_t$ instead of $\Sigma_t$.
\subsection*{Proving the identity}
In this part we are going to simply subtitute the RHS of \ref{eq:identity} into the LHS of the equation for $\psi(x,\mu,t;c)$ and show that it satisfies the same equation. After substitution and application of the chain rule we get:
\begin{align}
    \text{LHS} &= \frac{1}{v}\left[c(c-1)v\Sigma_t e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi}+c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t}\right] + \mu c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + \Sigma_t c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi} \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\left[\frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + \Sigma_t \tilde{\psi} + (c-1)\Sigma_t\tilde{\psi}\right] \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\left[\frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + c\Sigma_t \tilde{\psi}\right] \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\left[\frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \tilde{\psi}d\mu^\prime + \delta(x)\delta(t)\right] = \frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi}d\mu^\prime + c\delta(x)\delta(t)
\end{align}
where \ref{eq:tilde} was used in the last line, and also the fact that $f(t)\delta(t)$ = $f(0)\delta(t)$. The result is exactly the RHS of the equation for $\psi(x,\mu,t;c)$, and thus the identity is proven.

\section*{Question 2 - Derivation of the time-dependent planar scalar flux for a plane source}
The relation between a plane source and a point source as studied in class is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{pl}}(x) = 2\pi\int_{|x|}^{\infty} \phi_{\text{pt}}(r)rdr
\end{equation}
The solution taken from J. C. J. Paasschens is the point-source scalar flux $\phi_{\text{pt}}(r,t)$ for $c=1$, and therefore the planar-source scalar flux is given by the integration given above.
\begin{align}
    \phi_{\text{pl}}(x,t) &= 2\pi \int_{|x|}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{{4\pi r^2}}\delta(r-vt)rdr + {2\pi}\int_{|x|}^{\infty} \frac{{\left(1-r^2/v^2t^2\right)}^{1/8}}{\left(4\pi vt/(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} e^{-v\Sigma_t t}G\left(v\Sigma_t t\left[1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right]^{3/4}\right)\Theta(vt-r)rdr \\
    &= \frac{e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{2vt}\Theta(vt-|x|) + \Theta(vt-|x|) \frac{2\pi e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{\left(4\pi vt/(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} \int_{|x|}^{vt} \left(1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right)^{1/8} G\left(v\Sigma_t t\left[1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right]^{3/4}\right)rdr \\
    &\overset{{\xi = r/vt}}{=} \frac{e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{2vt}\Theta(vt-|x|) + \Theta(vt-|x|) \frac{2\pi e^{-v\Sigma_t t}(vt)^{1/2}}{\left(4\pi /(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} \int_{\frac{|x|}{vt}}^{1} \left(1-\xi^2\right)^{1/8} G\left(a\left[1-\xi^2\right]^{3/4}\right) \xi d\xi \\
    &\overset{\substack{u=1-\xi^2\\ w=au^{3/4}}}{=} \frac{e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{2vt}\left[1 + \sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{w_0}\sqrt{w}G(w)dw\right]\Theta(vt-|x|)
\end{align}


with $w_0 = a\left(1-\frac{x^2}{(vt)^2}\right)^{3/4}$, the powers combining as $\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{2}$ and the $\Sigma_t$ dependence cancelling in the last step (I used the assistance of AI in this algebric derivation). Finally, with the interpolation formula of Paasschens, $G(w) = e^{w}\sqrt{1+b/w}$ with $b=2.026$, the integral has a closed form:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{0}^{w_0}e^{w}\sqrt{w+b}\,dw = e^{w_0}\sqrt{w_0+b} - \sqrt{b} - \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{-b}\left[\text{erfi}\left(\sqrt{w_0+b}\right)-\text{erfi}\left(\sqrt{b}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
which \textbf{closes the derivation of the planar-source scalar flux for $c=1$.}
Of course the relation proved in Q1 can be used to get the solution for $c\neq 1$ using a simple scaling and multiplication by an exponential factor of the solution for $c=1$.

\section*{Question 3 - Classical and asymptotic diffusion against the exact solution}
\subsection*{Derivation of the time-dependent diffusion Green's function}
\label{sec:derivation}
The time-dependent diffusion equation is:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} - D\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \Sigma_a\phi = S(x,t) \label{eq:fluxform}
\end{equation}
Take $S = \delta(x)\delta(t)$ and multiply \eqref{eq:fluxform} by $v$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} - Dv\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + v\Sigma_a\phi = v\,\delta(x)\delta(t) \label{eq:vform}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 1: the pulse source is an initial condition.} Integrate \eqref{eq:vform} over
$t\in(0^-,0^+)$. The diffusion and absorption terms are bounded and contribute nothing in the
limit, so only the time derivative survives:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x,0^+) - \phi(x,0^-) = v\,\delta(x) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \phi(x,0^+) = v\,\delta(x)
\end{equation}
since nothing precedes the pulse. For $t>0$ the equation is source-free, so the problem is now
a pure initial-value problem (this is the same trick as in Assignment 1, Question 2)

\paragraph{Step 2: absorption factors out exactly.} As performed in the course, substituting
$\phi(x,t) = e^{-v\Sigma_a t}\,u(x,t)$ into \eqref{eq:vform} for $t>0$, the $-v\Sigma_a u$
produced by the time derivative cancels the absorption term identically and leaves the pure
heat equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = Dv\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
    \qquad u(x,0^+) = v\,\delta(x)
\end{equation}
Note this step is untouched by the sign of $1-c$: for $c>1$ the exponential grows instead of
decays, and nothing else changes.

\paragraph{Step 3: Fourier transform.} With
$\hat u(k,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u(x,t)e^{-ikx}dx$, the spatial derivative becomes
multiplication by $-k^2$ and the PDE becomes an ordinary differential equation in $t$ for each
$k$ separately:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\hat u}{dt} = -Dvk^2\,\hat u, \qquad \hat u(k,0^+) = v
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \hat u(k,t) = v\,e^{-Dvk^2t}
\end{equation}
the initial value being $v$ because the transform of $v\delta(x)$ is the constant $v$ - a
delta excites every wavenumber equally.

\paragraph{Step 4: invert.} Completing the square in the exponent,
$-Dvtk^2 + ikx = -Dvt\left(k - \frac{ix}{2Dvt}\right)^2 - \frac{x^2}{4Dvt}$, and using
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha k^2}dk = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$:
\begin{equation}
    u(x,t) = \frac{v}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-Dvk^2t}e^{ikx}dk
           = \frac{v}{\sqrt{4\pi Dvt}}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dvt)}
\end{equation}
Restoring the factor from Step 2 gives the Green's function:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;\phi_{\text{diff}}(x,t;c) = \frac{v\,e^{-(1-c)\Sigma_t v t}}{\sqrt{4\pi Dvt}}\,
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dvt}\right)\;}\label{eq:green}
\end{equation}
which in the units of this assignment, $\Sigma_t = v = 1$ and $D = D_0(c)$, is the form
implemented in \texttt{src/homework2/diffusion.py}:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;\phi_{\text{diff}}(x,t;c) = \frac{e^{-(1-c)t}}{\sqrt{4\pi D_0(c)\,t}}\,e^{-x^2/(4D_0(c)t)}\;}
\end{equation}

\begin{notebox}[A second route: Fourier in space, then Laplace in time][blue]
Define
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\phi}(k,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x,t)\,e^{-ikx}dx,
    \qquad
    \tilde{\phi}(k,s) = \int_{0^-}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}(k,t)\,e^{-st}dt
\end{equation}
and apply both to \eqref{eq:vform} term by term, with $\phi(x,0^-) = 0$:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} &\;\longrightarrow\; s\,\tilde{\phi} - \hat{\phi}(k,0^-) = s\,\tilde{\phi} \\
    -Dv\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} &\;\longrightarrow\; Dvk^2\,\tilde{\phi} \\
    v\Sigma_a\,\phi &\;\longrightarrow\; v\Sigma_a\,\tilde{\phi} \\
    v\,\delta(x)\delta(t) &\;\longrightarrow\; v
\end{align}
so that
\begin{gather}
    \left(s + Dvk^2 + v\Sigma_a\right)\tilde{\phi}(k,s) = v \\
    \tilde{\phi}(k,s) = \frac{v}{s + Dvk^2 + v\Sigma_a}
\end{gather}
a single simple pole at $s = -(Dvk^2 + v\Sigma_a)$, so with
$\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(s+\alpha)^{-1}\} = e^{-\alpha t}$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\phi}(k,t) = v\,e^{-\left(Dvk^2 + v\Sigma_a\right)t}
\end{equation}
and the Fourier inversion completes the square exactly as in Step 4:
\begin{align}
    \phi(x,t) &= \frac{v}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\left(Dvk^2 + v\Sigma_a\right)t}e^{ikx}dk \\
    &= \frac{v\,e^{-v\Sigma_a t}}{2\pi}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dvt)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-Dvt\left(k - \frac{ix}{2Dvt}\right)^2}dk \\
    &= \frac{v\,e^{-v\Sigma_a t}}{2\pi}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dvt)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{Dvt}}
     = \frac{v\,e^{-v\Sigma_a t}}{\sqrt{4\pi Dvt}}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dvt)}
\end{align}
which is \eqref{eq:green}, with $\Sigma_a = (1-c)\Sigma_t$.
\end{notebox}

\subsection*{Part 3(c): the numerical solution}
\label{sec:numerical}

Before discretising, the absorption is removed exactly as in Step 2: with
$\phi(x,t) = e^{-v\Sigma_a t}u(x,t)$ the term $v\Sigma_a\phi$ cancels identically and what is
left is the pure heat equation carrying the pulse as its initial condition:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = Dv\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
    \qquad u(x,0^+) = v\,\delta(x)
    \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
Since the equation and initial condition are even in $x$, so is the solution of \eqref{eq:heat}.
\paragraph{Truncation.} The solution is plotted in $[0,L]$ and is assumed to be zero at $x=L$. This truncation is justified because the spatial Gaussian from the analytic solution has variance $\sigma^2 = 2Dvt$, so taking
\begin{equation}
    L = x_{\max} + 8\sqrt{2Dv\,t_{\max}}
\end{equation}
places the truncation eight standard deviations beyond the last point to be plotted, where the
solution is of order $e^{-32}$ of its peak.

\paragraph{The grid, and the discrete pulse.} Take nodes $x_j = jh$ with $j = 0\ldots N$.
The spatial width is $h = L/N$, and time levels $t^n = n\Delta t$. On a node-centred grid the trapezoid weight of node
$0$ on the half line is $h/2$, and the full-line integral is twice the half-line one, so the
cell straddling the origin carries total weight $h$:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u(x,0)\,dx \;\approx\; 2\cdot\frac{h}{2}\,u_0^0 \;=\; h\,u_0^0
    \;\overset{!}{=}\; v
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    u_0^0 = \frac{v}{h}, \quad u_{j\ge1}^0 = 0
    \label{eq:ic}
\end{equation}
so the spike of height $v/h$ in the first cell is the correctly normalised delta - the
factor of two from the symmetry and the half-weight of the boundary node cancel.

\paragraph{The update.} Central second differences in space, evaluated at the old level, give
the explicit scheme. With $r = Dv\,\Delta t/h^2$,
\begin{align}
    u_j^{n+1} &= u_j^n + r\left(u_{j-1}^n - 2u_j^n + u_{j+1}^n\right), \qquad 1 \le j \le N-1
    \label{eq:ftcs} \\
    u_0^{n+1} &= u_0^n + 2r\left(u_1^n - u_0^n\right) \\
    u_N^{n+1} &= 0
\end{align}
the row at $j=0$ following from the symmetry condition written as the ghost value
$u_{-1} = u_1$.

\paragraph{Von Neuman Stability.} Substituting the Fourier mode $u_j^n = \xi^n e^{ij\theta}$ into
\eqref{eq:ftcs} gives the amplification factor
\begin{equation}
    \xi(\theta) = 1 + r\left(e^{-i\theta} - 2 + e^{i\theta}\right) = 1 - 4r\sin^2(\theta/2)
    \label{eq:stab}
\end{equation}
and $|\xi| \le 1$ for every $\theta$ requires $4r \le 2$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    r \le \frac{1}{2}
    \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
    \Delta t \le \frac{h^2}{2Dv}
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Conservation, as the check.} Let $M^n$ be the trapezoid mass on the half line, so
that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u\,dx \approx 2M^n$:
\begin{equation}
    M^n = h\left[\tfrac{1}{2}u_0^n + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}u_j^n + \tfrac{1}{2}u_N^n\right]
\end{equation}
Calculating $M^{n+1}$ using \eqref{eq:ftcs} and the boundary conditions gives
\begin{equation}
    M^{n+1} - M^n = r h\left(u_N^n - u_{N-1}^n\right) = -\,r h\,u_{N-1}^n
    \label{eq:conserve}
\end{equation}
So the scheme conserves particles to machine precision until the tail reaches the truncation,
and the only leak is through the far boundary, at a rate that is directly observable as
$u_{N-1}^n$. Restoring the prefactor, the check on the solver is
$\int\phi\,dx = e^{-v\Sigma_a t}$, the same identity already used on the exact solution.
\paragraph{Results.} The scheme is implemented in \texttt{src/homework2/solver.py} and run at
$N = 2000$ nodes, which puts $h \approx 0.022$ and $\Delta t \approx 6.5\times10^{-4}$, i.e.
about $2\times10^{4}$ sweeps to reach $t = 15$. Figure~\ref{fig:q3c_solver} places it over the
closed form it discretises and Figure~\ref{fig:q3c_error} measures what separates them. Three
results are worth quoting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservation is exact.} $\int\phi\,dx$ matches $e^{-(1-c)t}$ to between $0$
    and $1.1\times10^{-15}$ over $c = 0.6, 1.0, 1.5$ and $t = 1, 4, 15$ - round-off, not
    discretisation, as \eqref{eq:conserve} requires.
    \item \textbf{The scheme is second order.} The peak error at $t = 4$ falls
    $1.007\times10^{-3} \to 2.586\times10^{-4} \to 6.466\times10^{-5}$ as $N$ doubles from
    $500$ to $2000$: ratios of $3.89$ and $4.00$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Part 3(d): the numerical solutions against the exact one}
\label{sec:q3d}

Part 3(d) asks how far the numerical solution of 3(c) sits from the exact flux of Question 2.
The measured quantity is the pointwise relative error
\begin{equation}
    E(x,t;c) = \left|\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{diff}}(x,t)}{\phi_{\mathrm{exact}}(x,t)} - 1\right|
    \label{eq:relerr}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{\mathrm{diff}}$ is the march of \eqref{eq:ftcs} run once with $D = 1/3$ and once
with the asymptotic $D = D_0(c) = (1-c)\nu_0^2$, and $\phi_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is the Question 2
flux carried to $c \neq 1$ by the scaling \eqref{eq:identity}, evaluated from the exact series
$G$.

Two choices fix the domain of Figure~\ref{fig:q3d}. The exact solution is identically zero
beyond the causal front, so \eqref{eq:relerr} is cut at $0.98\,vt$, just inside it. And since
the front moves, the abscissa is the scaled position $x/vt$, which puts every time on one axis
with the front always at $1$.

\paragraph{What the figure measures.} Part 3(c) established that the scheme reproduces the
Green's function it discretises to $6.5\times10^{-5}$ at $N = 2000$, while Figure~\ref{fig:q3d}
spans $10^{-2}$ to $10^{4}$. The discretisation sits two to eight orders of magnitude below
everything plotted, so \textbf{what is measured here is the diffusion approximation itself, not
the solver}. The downward spikes are sign changes, where the error passes through zero.

\subsection*{Discussing the results}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Diffusion is a late-time limit} The relative error of both diffusion solutions decays with time, and the decay is faster the closer $c$ is to $1$.
    In addition, the quality of the approximation generally improves with time: the exact solution relaxes onto its diffusive shape only once a particle has scattered
    many times, and at $t = 1$ - one mean free time, one collision - there is nothing diffusive
    about it.

    \item \textbf{Once relaxed, $D_0(c)$ is the better coefficient, and by a large factor.} At $t = 15$
    the asymptotic error at the origin is $2.61\%$ against $23.88\%$ at $c=0.6$, $0.59\%$ against
    $8.39\%$ at $c=0.8$, $3.67\%$ against $10.59\%$ at $c=1.2$ and $4.85\%$ against $19.70\%$ at
    $c=1.5$ - a factor of four to fourteen. That is the payoff of the asymptotic coefficient:
    $D_0 = (1-c)\nu_0^2$ is built from the discrete eigenvalue that governs the exact solution at long times, whereas
    $1/3$ is not built from anything about the medium at all.
\end{enumerate}
