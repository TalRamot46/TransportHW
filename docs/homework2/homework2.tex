\section{Question 1 - Useful identity for the solution of the time-dependent slab-geometry Boltzmann equation}
In this question we are asked to prove the following identity:
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x,\mu,t;c) = ce^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\psi(cx,\mu,ct;1) \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
(in the units $v=\Sigma_t=1$ used by Paasschens the exponent is simply $-(1-c)t$; we keep the factor $v\Sigma_t$ so that the exponent is dimensionless).

We will do it in two parts. First, we will write down the equation that $\tilde{\psi}(x,\mu,t) \equiv \psi(cx,\mu,ct;1)$ satisfies, and then we will use it in order to prove that the product $ce^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi}$ satisfies the same equation as $\psi(x,\mu,t;c)$. Finally we check the initial condition, which is what fixes the prefactor $c$.

The two equations being related are the transport equation with scattering ratio $c$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} + \Sigma_t \psi = \frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \psi\, d\mu^\prime \label{eq:transport_c}
\end{equation}
and the same equation at $c=1$, written in its own variables $(X,\mu,T)$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial T} + \mu \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial X} + \Sigma_t \Psi = \frac{\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \Psi\, d\mu^\prime \label{eq:transport_1}
\end{equation}
\subsection{The equation for $\tilde{\psi}$}
With $\tilde{\psi}(x,\mu,t) = \Psi(cx,\mu,ct)$, i.e. $X=cx$ and $T=ct$, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} = c\left.\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial T}\right|_{(cx,ct)}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \left.\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial T}\right|_{(cx,ct)} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial t},
    \qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad
    \left.\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial X}\right|_{(cx,ct)} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial x}.
\end{equation}
Substituting these into \eqref{eq:transport_1} and multiplying through by $c$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + c\Sigma_t \tilde{\psi} = \frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \tilde{\psi}\, d\mu^\prime \label{eq:transport_tilde}
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:transport_tilde} with \eqref{eq:transport_c}, the two scattering sources already agree; the only difference is that $\tilde{\psi}$ is removed at the rate $c\Sigma_t$ instead of $\Sigma_t$. The exponential factor in \eqref{eq:identity} exists precisely to make up this deficit of $(1-c)\Sigma_t$.
\subsection{Proving the identity}
In this part we are going to simply substitute the RHS of \eqref{eq:identity} into the LHS of \eqref{eq:transport_c} and show that it satisfies the same equation. Note that $\tilde{\psi}$ is already a function of the unscaled variables $(x,\mu,t)$, so $\partial_t$ and $\partial_x$ act on it directly and no further chain rule is applied:
\begin{align}
    \text{LHS} &= \frac{1}{v}\left[-c(1-c)v\Sigma_t e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi}+c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t}\right] + \mu c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + \Sigma_t c\, e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi} \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\left[\frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + \Sigma_t \tilde{\psi} - (1-c)\Sigma_t\tilde{\psi}\right] \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\left[\frac{1}{v}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial x} + c\Sigma_t \tilde{\psi}\right] \\
    &= c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\,\frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \tilde{\psi}\, d\mu^\prime
    = \frac{c\Sigma_t}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \left[c e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\tilde{\psi}\right] d\mu^\prime
\end{align}
which is exactly \eqref{eq:transport_c}, as required.
\subsection{The prefactor $c$ and the initial condition}
Equation \eqref{eq:transport_c} is linear and homogeneous, so it is satisfied by any constant multiple of a solution; the PDE by itself therefore cannot determine the prefactor $c$. It is fixed instead by the source. For the plane-pulse Green's function the initial condition is $\psi(x,\mu,0^+) = \frac{v}{2}\delta(x)$ for \textit{every} value of $c$, and evaluating the RHS of \eqref{eq:identity} at $t\to 0^+$, using $\delta(cx)=\delta(x)/|c|$ for $c>0$, gives
\begin{equation}
    c\,e^{0}\,\psi(cx,\mu,0^+;1) = c\cdot \frac{v}{2}\delta(cx) = c\cdot\frac{v}{2}\cdot\frac{\delta(x)}{c} = \frac{v}{2}\delta(x).
\end{equation}
So the prefactor $c$ is exactly the Jacobian of the rescaling $x\mapsto cx$ acting on the source delta, and with both the equation and the initial condition matched the identity is proved. $\blacksquare$
\section{Question 2 - Derivation of the time-dependent planar scalar flux for a plane source}
The relation between a plane source and a point source as studied in class is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{pl}}(x) = 2\pi\int_{|x|}^{\infty} \phi_{\text{pt}}(r)rdr
\end{equation}
The solution taken from J. C. J. Paasschens is the point-source result $P(r,t)$ for $c=1$. Note that Paasschens' $P$ is normalised as a probability density, $\int P\, d\mathbf{r} = 1$ (his Eq.~(34) summed over the collision number $N$), so it is a number density and the scalar flux is $\phi = vP$; we compute the density $n_{\text{pl}}(x,t)$ below and multiply by $v$ at the end. Throughout we abbreviate
\begin{equation}
    a \equiv v\Sigma_t t .
\end{equation}
Applying the superposition relation to both the uncollided and the collided part of $P$:
\begin{align}
    n_{\text{pl}}(x,t) &= 2\pi \int_{|x|}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-a}}{{4\pi r^2}}\delta(r-vt)\,r\,dr + 2\pi\int_{|x|}^{\infty} \frac{{\left(1-r^2/v^2t^2\right)}^{1/8}}{\left(4\pi vt/(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} e^{-a}G\left(a\left[1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right]^{3/4}\right)\Theta(vt-r)\,r\,dr \\
    &= \frac{e^{-a}}{2vt}\Theta(vt-|x|) + \Theta(vt-|x|)\, \frac{2\pi e^{-a}}{\left(4\pi vt/(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} \int_{|x|}^{vt} \left(1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right)^{1/8} G\left(a\left[1-\frac{r^2}{v^2t^2}\right]^{3/4}\right)r\,dr \\
    &= \frac{e^{-a}}{2vt}\Theta(vt-|x|) + \Theta(vt-|x|)\, \frac{2\pi e^{-a}(vt)^{1/2}}{\left(4\pi /(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}} \underbrace{\int_{\frac{|x|}{vt}}^{1} \left(1-\xi^2\right)^{1/8} G\left(a\left[1-\xi^2\right]^{3/4}\right) \xi \,d\xi}_{\displaystyle \equiv\, I}
\end{align}
Note that the collided term carries $\Theta(vt-|x|)$ and not $\mathrm{sign}(vt-|x|)$: when $|x|>vt$ the factor $\Theta(vt-r)$ annihilates the integrand for every $r\geq|x|$, so the term vanishes rather than changing sign.
\subsection{Reduction of the integral $I$}
The integral $I$ looks forbidding, but it is elementary. Substituting first $u = 1-\xi^2$, so that $\xi\,d\xi = -\tfrac{1}{2}du$ and the limits become $u:0\to u_0 \equiv 1 - x^2/(vt)^2$, and then $w = a\,u^{3/4}$, so that $u=(w/a)^{4/3}$, $du = \tfrac{4}{3}a^{-4/3}w^{1/3}dw$ and $u^{1/8}=a^{-1/6}w^{1/6}$, the fractional powers combine as $\tfrac16+\tfrac13=\tfrac12$ and
\begin{equation}
    I = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{u_0} u^{1/8} G\!\left(a u^{3/4}\right)du = \frac{2}{3a^{3/2}}\int_0^{w_0} \sqrt{w}\,G(w)\,dw,
    \qquad w_0 \equiv a\left(1-\frac{x^2}{(vt)^2}\right)^{3/4}.
\end{equation}
The integrable $(vt-r)^{-1/4}$ endpoint singularity of the original integrand is removed by this change of variable, so no special quadrature is needed. Inserting $I$ back, the prefactor collapses and the $\Sigma_t$ dependence cancels completely,
\begin{equation}
    2\pi\,\frac{e^{-a}(vt)^{1/2}}{\left(4\pi/(3\Sigma_t)\right)^{3/2}}\cdot\frac{2}{3a^{3/2}} = \frac{e^{-a}}{2vt}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}},
\end{equation}
so that both terms share a common prefactor and
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;n_{\text{pl}}(x,t) = \frac{e^{-v\Sigma_t t}}{2vt}\left[1 + \sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\int_0^{w_0}\sqrt{w}\,G(w)\,dw\right]\Theta(vt-|x|)\;},
    \qquad \phi_{\text{pl}} = v\,n_{\text{pl}} .
\end{equation}
\subsection{Closed form}
With Paasschens' interpolation formula (his Eq.~(36b)), $G(w)\approx e^{w}\sqrt{1+b/w}$ with $b=2.026$, the $\sqrt{w}$ produced by the substitution rationalises the square root exactly:
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{w}\,G(w) = e^{w}\sqrt{w+b},
\end{equation}
and the integral is elementary. Using $\int e^{s}\sqrt{s}\,ds = e^{s}\sqrt{s} - \tfrac{1}{2}\int e^{s}s^{-1/2}ds$ with $\int e^{s}s^{-1/2}ds = \sqrt{\pi}\,\mathrm{erfi}(\sqrt{s})$ and $s=w+b$,
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{w_0} e^{w}\sqrt{w+b}\,dw = e^{w_0}\sqrt{w_0+b} - \sqrt{b} - \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{-b}\left[\mathrm{erfi}\!\left(\sqrt{w_0+b}\right) - \mathrm{erfi}\!\left(\sqrt{b}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
For numerical evaluation it is better to write this with the Dawson function $D(z) = e^{-z^2}\int_0^z e^{y^2}dy$, using $\mathrm{erfi}(z) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{z^2}D(z)$, which factors out the exponential growth:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{w_0} e^{w}\sqrt{w+b}\,dw = e^{w_0}\left[\sqrt{w_0+b}-D\!\left(\sqrt{w_0+b}\right)\right] - \left[\sqrt{b}-D\!\left(\sqrt{b}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
so that, since $w_0 \le a$ and therefore $e^{-a}e^{w_0}\le 1$, the final result contains no large or cancelling terms:
\begin{equation}
    n_{\text{pl}}(x,t) = \frac{\Theta(vt-|x|)}{2vt}\left\{e^{-a} + \sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\left(e^{-(a-w_0)}\left[\sqrt{w_0+b}-D\!\left(\sqrt{w_0+b}\right)\right] - e^{-a}\left[\sqrt{b}-D\!\left(\sqrt{b}\right)\right]\right)\right\}
\end{equation}
If the exact $G$ is preferred over the interpolation, the same reduction integrates Paasschens' series term by term:
\begin{equation}
    G(x) = 8(3x)^{-3/2}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}N+\tfrac{3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}N\right)}\frac{x^N}{N!}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \int_0^{w_0}\sqrt{w}\,G(w)\,dw = \frac{8}{3^{3/2}}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}N+\tfrac{3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}N\right)}\frac{w_0^{\,N}}{N\cdot N!}.
\end{equation}
\subsection{Checks}
At the causal front $|x|\to vt$ we have $w_0\to 0$ and the bracket tends to $\sqrt{b}-\sqrt{b}=0$, so $n_{\text{pl}}\to e^{-a}/(2vt)$, the uncollided value alone, as it must. Integrating the result over $x$ with the exact series $G$ gives $\int_{-vt}^{vt}n_{\text{pl}}\,dx = 1$ to eight digits at $v\Sigma_t t = 0.3,1,3,10,30$, i.e. exact particle conservation for $c=1$; with the interpolation formula the same integral gives $1.0008$--$1.0147$, consistent with the few-percent accuracy Paasschens quotes for Eq.~(36). At large $t$ and $x=0$ the result approaches the planar diffusion solution $(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}$ with $D=v/(3\Sigma_t)$.

Finally, the relation proved in Q1 gives the solution for $c\neq 1$ from the $c=1$ result above by a simple scaling and multiplication by an exponential factor,
\begin{equation}
    n_{\text{pl}}(x,t;c) = c\,e^{-(1-c)v\Sigma_t t}\, n_{\text{pl}}(cx,ct;1),
\end{equation}
so the planar Green's function only ever has to be constructed once.
